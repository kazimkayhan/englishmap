EnglishMap — English for Dari & Hazaragi Speakers

Hazaragi Learning Notes — Study Tips & Cultural Considerations

Specific guidance for Hazara women learning English

Identity & Language Learning

You Are Multilingual (Already!)

If you speak Hazaragi and understand Dari (even partially), you're already bilingual or bidialectal. Adding English makes you trilingual.

Benefits of being multilingual:

  • Faster pattern recognition
  • Better cognitive flexibility
  • Cultural bridge-building skills
  • More job/study opportunities

Your Hazaragi background is an asset, not a barrier.

Cultural Considerations

Learning English as a Hazara Woman

Historical Context

Hazara people have faced systemic educational barriers:

  • Discrimination and marginalization
  • Geographic isolation (mountainous Hazarajat)
  • Interrupted education (especially for girls)
  • Conflict and displacement

Result: Many Hazara girls had limited or no formal schooling — through no fault of their own.

Your Education, Your Terms

You don't need permission to learn.

  • Learning English is not disrespectful to Hazaragi culture
  • Education honors your ancestors (who valued knowledge)
  • English opens doors without erasing your identity
  • You can be proud Hazara and fluent in English

Balancing Tradition & Modernity

Traditional ValueHow It Supports English Learning
Respect for eldersLearn to communicate Hazara history and culture to the world
Community solidarityEnglish helps connect Hazara diaspora globally
Oral storytellingTranslate Hazara oral traditions; keep them alive
ResilienceLanguage learning requires the same perseverance Hazara people have always shown
Education as honorYour education honors your family, even if done privately

Learning English doesn't make you less Hazara. It makes you a Hazara woman with more tools.

Hazaragi Thinking Patterns & English Learning

Strength 1: Narrative Thinking

Hazaragi culture values stories (oral history, family tales, community memory).

Use this for English:

  • Learn vocabulary through stories, not lists
  • Practice speaking by telling simple stories: "Yesterday I…"
  • Read short English stories and retell them in your own words
  • Connect grammar to narrative: "If I go… then I will…"

Example Practice:

Think of a Hazaragi folk tale you know. Try to summarize it in simple English:

Long ago, there was a brave girl. She lived in the mountains. One day, she found a lost child. She helped the child find home.

Strength 2: Community Orientation

Hazaragi culture is collective, not individualistic.

Use this for English:

  • Study with a sister, cousin, or friend (even 15 min/day together)
  • Share vocabulary with someone else learning
  • Teach what you learn (teaching strengthens your own knowledge)
  • Think of English as a tool to help your community, not just yourself

Example:

"I learn English so I can translate health information for my mother."

Strength 3: Practical Skills

Hazara people are known for practical problem-solving (agriculture, crafts, trade).

Use this for English:

  • Focus on useful English first (health, safety, online work, communication)
  • Skip abstract theory; prioritize real-life skills
  • Learn English through practical activities: cooking (recipe vocab), shopping (money vocab), health (body parts, symptoms)

Overcoming Specific Challenges

Challenge 1: Limited Formal Schooling

Reality: Many Hazara girls had little or interrupted schooling.

Impact on English learning:

  • Unfamiliar with "school" learning style (grammar rules, exercises)
  • Reading in Perso-Arabic script may be weak
  • Latin alphabet is brand new

Solution:

  1. Start with spoken English — listening and speaking
    • You learned Hazaragi by listening; use the same method for English
  2. Treat Latin alphabet as a completely new system
    • Don't compare to Perso-Arabic; learn from scratch
    • Practice 10 min/day: Alphabet
  3. Use audio resources heavily
    • Podcasts, YouTube, voice apps
    • Listen → repeat → listen again
  4. Grammar comes later
    • Learn useful phrases first: "I need help." "Where is the bathroom?"
    • Add grammar rules gradually

Challenge 2: Shame About "Late Start"

Feeling: "I'm too old. I should have learned English in school."

Reality: Many successful English learners started as adults. Age is not the barrier — consistency is the key.

Solution:

  1. Reframe the narrative:

    • Not: "I'm behind."
    • Instead: "I'm starting now, which is brave."

  2. Compare to yesterday, not to others

    • Track small wins: "Last week I didn't know 'thank you,' now I do."

  3. Remember: Adults learn differently (better in some ways!)

    • Better discipline
    • Clearer goals
    • More life experience to connect to

Challenge 3: Juggling Home Responsibilities

Reality: Hazara women often have significant family/home responsibilities.

Impact: Hard to find 1-2 hours for study.

Solution:

  1. 30 minutes is enough
    • 15 min morning + 15 min evening = progress
  2. Integrate English into daily tasks
    • Listen to English while cooking
    • Label household items in English (stick notes: door, window, table)
    • Think in English: "I am washing dishes. The water is warm."
  3. Study when children study (if you have kids)
    • Parallel study time
    • Model learning behavior

Study Strategies Tailored to Hazaragi Speakers

Strategy 1: Use Both Languages in Notes

When learning new English words, write Hazaragi + English (skip Dari if it's not your natural thinking language).

Example vocabulary note:

EnglishHazaragiExample
waterآو āwI drink water — من آو می‌نوشم
sunآفتو aftowThe sun is hot — آفتو گرم است
houseکور korMy house is small — کور من کوچک است

Strategy 2: Hazaragi Sentence → English Sentence

Practice translating your own thoughts:

Hazaragi (think):

Man engelīsī mī-khām یاد بگیرم.

English (say aloud):

"I want to learn English."

Hazaragi:

Mār-am dar kor ast.

English:

"My mother is at home."

Do this 5 times/day with different sentences.

Strategy 3: Listening in Context

Hazaragi oral culture = strong listening skills

Use this:

  • Listen to English before reading transcripts
  • Guess meaning from context (like you do in Hazaragi conversations)
  • Replay short clips 3-5 times before looking up words
  • Mimic the speaker's rhythm and intonation

Recommended: BBC Learning English — slow, clear, free

Strategy 4: Record Yourself Daily

Hazaragi speakers: You're used to oral communication.

Practice:

  • Record 30 seconds in English every day
  • Topics: "My day," "What I ate," "The weather," "My family"
  • Listen the next day — note one thing to improve
  • Re-record the same topic after 1 week — hear your progress

Motivational Reminders for Hazara Learners

1. Your Ancestors Valued Knowledge

Hazara culture has always respected education and learning, even when access was denied.

Your English learning honors that tradition.

2. You're Not Alone

Millions of Hazara women globally are learning English, often in difficult circumstances.

Every word you learn, someone else is learning too.

3. Small Daily Steps = Big Results

Hazaragi proverb (adapted):

"آب قطره قطره دریا می‌شود" (Drop by drop, water becomes a river.)

English learning:

Word by word, you become fluent.

4. You Can Restart Anytime

Interruptions are normal.

  • War, displacement, family crises — these happen
  • English learning can wait when safety/survival is priority
  • The book will be here when you return

No shame in breaks. Only pride in returning.

Hazaragi-Specific Grammar Tips

Tip 1: English Needs Subject Pronouns

Hazaragi: Mī-berem. (verb alone = "I go")

English: Must say "I go" (subject required)

Practice: Add I, you, he, she, we, they to every sentence when translating from Hazaragi.

Tip 2: English Word Order is Strict (SVO)

Hazaragi: Flexible SOV

English: Must be SVO

Hazaragi thinking:

Man ketow (O) mī-khōnam (V). (I book read.)

English requires:

I (S) read (V) a book (O).

Practice: When translating from Hazaragi, rearrange: Subject first, then verb, then object.

Tip 3: Articles (a, an, the) Are Required

Hazaragi: No articles

English: Almost every noun needs a, an, or the

Hazaragi:

Ketow dar mēz ast. (Book on table is.)

English:

The book is on the table. (Articles added)

Practice: Every time you say a noun in English, ask: "Does it need a/an/the?"

Long-Term Goals: Why English Matters for Hazara Women

Personal Goals

  • Access global information (health, science, education)
  • Consume media and literature in English
  • Communicate with Hazara diaspora worldwide (many speak English as bridge language)

Professional Goals

  • Remote work (freelancing, virtual assistance, translation)
  • Online teaching (teach Dari/Hazaragi to others, earn income)
  • Healthcare, development, or international NGO work

Community Goals

  • Document Hazara history and culture in English (preserve and share)
  • Translate important information to/from Hazaragi
  • Advocate for Hazara rights and representation globally
  • Teach future generations (your children, nieces, community kids)

Political Goals (Long-Term)

  • Hazara women's voices in English-language media
  • English as a tool for activism and visibility
  • Countering stereotypes and misinformation about Hazara people

Your English is not just for you. It's for your people.
