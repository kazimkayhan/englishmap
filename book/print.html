Safety & Privacy Guide

For young women studying English at home in Afghanistan

Learning English is your right. This guide helps you study safely, privately, and without unnecessary risk.

Physical Safety

Choosing Your Study Space

Safe spaces:

  • ✅ Private room with a door you can close
  • ✅ Quiet corner where you won't be interrupted
  • ✅ Shared space during times when you feel comfortable
  • ✅ Any place where you can focus without fear

Tips:

  • Use headphones for listening practice
  • Keep your study materials in a discrete folder or app
  • If questioned, simple answers work: "I'm learning a skill" or "I'm studying for my future"
  • You don't owe detailed explanations to anyone

Recording Yourself (Speaking Practice)

Voice recording is essential for pronunciation practice, but be cautious:

Safe practices:

  • ✅ Use voice memos app with a strong passcode/lock
  • ✅ Store recordings in a private folder
  • ✅ Delete old recordings after reviewing (weekly cleanup)
  • ✅ Never upload recordings to public platforms
  • ✅ Avoid saying your full name or location in recordings

What to record:

  • Practice sentences: "Hello. I study English."
  • Reading passages from this book
  • Describing simple topics: "My daily routine" (generic)

What NOT to record:

  • Personal identifying information
  • Political opinions or sensitive topics
  • Your location or family details

Digital Safety

Device & App Security

Essential protections:

  1. Strong passcode on your phone/tablet (not 0000 or 1234)
  2. Biometric lock (fingerprint/face) if available
  3. Private browsing mode when using shared devices
  4. Clear browser history after study sessions on shared devices

Learning Apps & Websites

Trusted sources (free & safe):

  • This book (offline mdBook)
  • BBC Learning English (reputable)
  • Duolingo (privacy-conscious)
  • YouTube channels from known institutions (no account needed)

Red flags (avoid):

  • Apps requesting excessive permissions (contacts, camera, microphone for a vocabulary app?)
  • "Free trials" requiring credit card info
  • Apps from unknown developers
  • Platforms asking for real name, photo, and location for basic use

Account Safety

When creating accounts:

  • Use a username, not your real name
  • Create a separate email for learning apps (e.g., Gmail with pseudonym)
  • Strong, unique passwords (write them in a hidden notebook or password manager)
  • Skip optional fields (real name, phone, photo)
  • Never share personal information in profile or bio

Two-factor authentication (2FA):

  • Enable it if the platform offers it
  • Use authenticator apps (Google Authenticator, Authy) rather than SMS if possible
  • Protects your account even if password is guessed

Language Exchange & Online Practice

Safer Options

  • ✅ Text-based platforms (no video/voice)
  • ✅ Apps with reporting and blocking features
  • ✅ Group learning communities (not 1-on-1 chats)
  • ✅ Platforms with moderation (Hello Talk, Tandem have reports/blocks)

What NOT to Share

  • ❌ Real name or nickname others can trace
  • ❌ Location (city, neighborhood, school, landmarks)
  • ❌ Photos of yourself or family
  • ❌ Phone number or personal social media
  • ❌ Daily schedule or routines
  • ❌ Political or religious views (even if asked)

Red Flags — End Conversation Immediately If:

  • Someone asks where you live (city/country is max)
  • Requests move from app to WhatsApp/Telegram right away
  • Asks for photos or video calls
  • Sends inappropriate messages
  • Pressures you to meet in person
  • Shows too much interest in your personal life
  • Makes you feel uncomfortable in any way

Action: Block, report, and move on. You owe no explanation.

Emotional Safety

Managing Shame & Guilt

Remember:

  • Learning is not forbidden by any religion — all major faiths encourage education
  • You have the right to learn and grow
  • Taking breaks is normal, not failure
  • Missing days doesn't erase your progress
  • You are not "behind" — you are on your own path

Dealing with Discouragement

When you feel:

  • "I'm not smart enough" → False. You can learn any skill with practice.
  • "It's too late for me" → False. Adults learn languages successfully every day.
  • "I'll never be fluent" → Fluency is a journey, not a destination. Progress counts.
  • "I've forgotten everything after a break" → Review is faster than learning. You'll catch up quickly.

Action:

  • Read Building Confidence
  • Review your Progress Tracker — see how far you've come
  • Restart with 10 minutes today — that's enough

Comparison & Social Media

Avoid:

  • Comparing yourself to fluent speakers online (they have different histories)
  • Following "polyglot influencers" who make you feel bad
  • Perfectionism — "If I can't study 2 hours, I won't study at all" (wrong mindset)

Practice:

  • Compare yourself to yesterday's you
  • Celebrate small wins: learned 5 words, spoke 3 sentences, studied 15 minutes
  • Unfollow accounts that make you feel inadequate

Privacy in This Book

What This Book Does NOT Do

  • ❌ No tracking or analytics
  • ❌ No account required
  • ❌ No data collection
  • ❌ No internet needed after initial load
  • ❌ No ads or third-party scripts

What This Book DOES

  • ✅ Works completely offline
  • ✅ Stored locally on your device
  • ✅ No record of what you read or when
  • ✅ Private search (nothing leaves your device)

Keeping This Book Private

  • Bookmark as "Study Notes" or a generic name
  • Add to home screen with a discrete icon (mobile)
  • Use private/incognito browser if sharing device
  • Clear cache/history on shared devices

Emergency Protocol

If Someone Finds Your Study Materials

Stay calm. Options:

  1. Minimize:

    • "It's just English vocabulary for reading."
    • "I'm learning to read instructions better."
    • "It's for work/online tasks someday."

  2. Redirect:

    • Change subject
    • Offer to help them with something
    • Close the app calmly (don't panic)

  3. Hide in Plain Sight:

    • Keep book in a folder named "Recipes" or "Notes"
    • Mix study files with other documents
    • Use apps that look like games or utilities

If You Feel Unsafe

  • Stop studying temporarily — your safety comes first
  • Delete apps if needed (you can reinstall later)
  • Study in shorter bursts when alone
  • Consider less obvious study materials (e.g., listening looks like music, reading looks like browsing)

Resources for Safety

If you need help with digital safety or privacy:

  • Access Now (digital security hotline)
  • Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) guides
  • Internews (safety resources for women)

Long-Term Privacy Habits

1. Regular Cleanups

  • Delete old voice recordings weekly
  • Clear browser history on shared devices
  • Remove apps you no longer use
  • Check app permissions quarterly

2. Separate Identities

  • Learning account ≠ personal account
  • Different passwords for different purposes
  • Use pseudonyms consistently

3. Trust Your Instincts

  • If something feels wrong, stop
  • If someone makes you uncomfortable, block them
  • If an app feels sketchy, delete it
  • Your gut is usually right

Specific Safety Scenarios

Scenario 1: Shared Family Phone

Strategy:

  • Study in private browsing mode
  • Clear history after each session
  • Use apps that can be hidden or password-protected
  • Alternatively, use this book offline without leaving traces

Scenario 2: Limited Privacy at Home

Strategy:

  • Study during times when you're alone
  • Use headphones always (looks like listening to music)
  • Keep screen angled away from doors/windows
  • Have a cover activity ready to switch to (recipes, news, games)

Scenario 3: Public Wi-Fi

Strategy:

  • Download lessons when on home Wi-Fi
  • Use offline mode in public
  • Avoid logging into accounts on public networks
  • Use VPN if available and legal in your area

Balancing Safety & Learning

Your education matters. Your safety matters more.

If you must choose between:

  • Studying openly but at risk → Choose safety, study discreetly
  • Perfect practice vs. safe practice → Choose safe
  • Recording yourself vs. staying silent → Silent practice is still practice

Alternative low-profile study methods:

  • Read silently (still effective)
  • Write vocabulary in a regular notebook (looks like any notes)

Core Grammar

Stage 1 · Months 1–3 (with foundations)

Learning goals

  1. Use verb be (am, is, are) correctly
  2. Use have / has for possession and facts
  3. Build present simple sentences (habits and facts)
  4. Ask yes/no and wh- questions
  5. Use negatives (don't, doesn't, not)
  6. Use there is / there are

Why this skill matters

Grammar is the skeleton of English. With these patterns, you can describe your life, ask questions, and stop guessing word order.

Learn patterns, not single sentences. Swap one word — make a new sentence.

Level focus

Stage 1 (A1–A2).

Step-by-step lessons

Lesson 1 — Subject + be (20 min)

SubjectBeExample
IamI am tired.
Youyou areYou are kind.
He / she / itisShe is at home.
We / theyareWe are students.

Negative: add not

  • I am not busy.
  • She is not here.

Questions: swap order

  • Are you fine? → Yes, I am. / No, I am not.
  • Is he home?

Dari note: Dari often drops “to be” in present. In English, do not drop it: say I am fine, not I fine.

Lesson 2 — Have / has (20 min)

Use have and has to show possession (I have a book) or some states (I have time / She has a problem).

SubjectFormExample
I / you / we / theyhaveI have a phone.
He / she / ithasShe has a sister.

Negative:

  • I do not have (don't have) Wi-Fi today.
  • He does not have (doesn't have) class on Friday.

Questions:

  • Do you have a quiet room? → Yes, I do. / No, I don't.
  • Does she have your number? → Yes, she does.

Contrast with be:

  • I am tired. (state — adjective)
  • I have time. (possession / availability)
  • She is a student. (identity)
  • She has a book. (possession)

Dari note: داشتن (dâštan — to have) works differently. English uses have/has in present, not a separate verb like am/is.

Pattern practice:

  • I have + noun → I have a notebook.
  • She has + noun → She has courage.
  • Do you have + noun? → Do you have internet?

Lesson 3 — Present simple (20 min)

Use for habits and facts.

Negative:

  • I do not (don't) study at night.
  • He does not (doesn't) study at night.

Questions:

  • Do you study English? → Yes, I do.
  • Does she study English? → Yes, she does.

You are no longer “studying English.” You are using English to live and think.

Level focus

Stage 2 (B1 → B2 bridge).

Prerequisite: Solid Stage 1 — present tense, 300+ words, basic listening.

Step-by-step lessons

Lesson 1 — Past simple: regular verbs (20 min)

Form: verb + -ed (walk → walked)

AffirmativeNegativeQuestion
I walkedI did not walkDid you walk?
She studiedShe did not studyDid she study?

Spelling rules:

  • stop → stopped (double consonant)
  • try → tried (y → ied)
  • play → played

Examples:

Yesterday I studied English for forty minutes.
Last year I started learning at home.
She helped her sister with homework.

Was/were + verb-ing — action in progress in the past

Lesson 7 — Connectors and storytelling (20 min)

Timeline words: first, then, after that, later, finally

Contrast: however, although, but

Cause: because, since, so

Mini story (90 sec spoken):

First, I was afraid to speak. Then I recorded my voice every week. After that, I understood more listening clips. Finally, I spoke for two minutes without stopping. Although I still make mistakes, I continue.

Lesson 8 — Fluency bridges (15 min)

Fillers (natural, not too many):

  • Well, … / Let me see, … / I mean, …

Rephrase when stuck:

Sorry, let me say that another way. …
What I want to say is, …

Practice: 2-minute talk on a challenge I overcame.

Examples

Past + opinion paragraph:

Last month I had a difficult week. I did not study for three days because I was tired. However, I started again on Monday. I think consistency matters more than perfection. I am going to study thirty minutes daily this month. I believe small steps change lives.

Advanced Skills (B2 → C2)

Stage 3 → Stage 4 · Months 9–24+

Learning goals

  1. Build complex sentences (clauses, conditionals)
  2. Control register (formal vs informal)
  3. Write and speak professionally (email, presentations)
  4. Use hedging and nuance (maybe, tend to, it appears)
  5. Argue a position clearly and politely

Why this skill matters

Advanced English is not “more grammar rules.” It is precision, respect, and power — in school applications, remote work, advocacy, and global conversation.

You have something to say. Advanced English helps the world hear it clearly.

Level focus

Stage 3–4 (B2 → C1 → C2).

Step-by-step lessons

Lesson 1 — Relative clauses (20 min)

Who — people · which — things · that — both (informal)

The woman who teaches online inspires me.
The course which I took was free.
The book that I read was difficult.

Reduced: The woman teaching online… (advanced style)

Lesson 2 — Conditionals (25 min)

TypeStructureExample
ZeroIf + present, presentIf you heat ice, it melts.
FirstIf + present, willIf I practice, I will improve.
SecondIf + past, wouldIf I had time, I would study more.
ThirdIf + past perfect, would haveIf I had started earlier, I would have learned more.

Your life:

If I study daily, I will reach B2.
If I had fluent English now, I would apply for more scholarships.

Lesson 3 — Passive voice (15 min)

Be + past participle — focus on action/object

English is taught online.
The message was sent yesterday.
Mistakes are made — that is normal.

Lesson 4 — Register: formal vs informal (20 min)

InformalFormal
HiDear / Hello
Thanks a lotThank you for your assistance
I wantI would like
ASAPas soon as possible
kidschildren

Rule: Job, university, unknown adults → formal. Friends, sisters → informal.

Lesson 5 — Hedging and nuance (15 min)

Avoid sounding too absolute in academic/professional speech:

HedgeExample
may, mightThis may help learners.
tend toWomen tend to face more barriers.
it seems / appearsIt appears that practice works.
in many casesIn many cases, small groups help.

Lesson 6 — Argument structure (25 min)

  1. Claim — main point
  2. Evidence — fact or example
  3. Counter — other view (optional)
  4. Reply — why your claim still holds
  5. Conclusion

Topic: Education should be available to all women.

Claim: Every woman deserves access to education. Evidence: Studies show education improves health and income. Counter: Some say resources are limited. Reply: Long-term investment in women strengthens communities. Conclusion: Restrictions harm society; education is a right.

Lesson 7 — Professional email (20 min)

Structure:

  • Subject line (clear)
  • Greeting (Dear Ms. … / Dear Team)
  • Purpose (first paragraph)
  • Details (bullet points if needed)
  • Polite close (Thank you for your consideration)
  • Signature (name, optional contact)

Lesson 8 — Presentations (20 min)

Outline (5–7 min talk):

  1. Hook — question or short story
  2. Problem
  3. Your main points (3 bullets)
  4. Solution or hope
  5. Call to action — what listener can do
  6. Thank you

Practice: Record on phone; watch once; note filler words to reduce.

Examples

Formal sentence:

I am writing to inquire about the registration deadline for the online certificate program.

Nuanced opinion:

Although progress takes time, it seems that consistent self-study can lead to strong results, especially when learners face limited classroom access.

Practice tasks

  1. Write 5 sentences with who/which/that.
  2. Write 3 first conditional + 2 second conditional sentences about your goals.
  3. Draft one professional email (real or practice).
  4. Record 3-minute presentation outline on why I learn English.

Self-check questions

  1. When use who vs which?
  2. Give an example of first vs second conditional.
  3. What is hedging and why use it?
  4. Name five parts of an argument.
  5. What is different in formal email greeting?

Study Strategies for Long-Term Success

All stages · Read early; revisit after breaks

Learning goals

  1. Build consistency without burnout
  2. Restart after gaps without shame
  3. Create a safe study environment
  4. Use motivation tools that work in hard times
  5. Study with a sister circle safely (optional)

Why this skill matters

Skills fade without habits. Strategies keep you learning when life interrupts school, internet, or confidence.

Discipline is remembering what you want. Courage is studying anyway.

Level focus

All stages — especially after breaks.

Step-by-step lessons

Lesson 1 — The 3 pillars (15 min)

PillarAction
TimeSame window most days (even 15 min)
MethodRotate skills (V/G/L/S/R/W)
MindsetKind self-talk

Missing one pillar? Fix the weakest — usually mindset after a hard week.

Lesson 2 — Motivation that lasts (20 min)

Weak motivation: “I must be fluent in 3 months.”
Strong motivation: “Today I will learn 10 words and use them.”

Write your why (keep private):

  • For my education
  • For remote work
  • To read books
  • To help my family
  • To prove I can

Read your why every Sunday.

Visual wins: checklist in 16-tracker.md — tick marks release dopamine.

Lesson 3 — Recovery after interruption (15 min)

If you stopped 1–7 days:

  1. Do not study double time to “catch up”
  2. Open tracker — read last win
  3. Study 15 minutes today only
  4. Return to normal plan tomorrow

If you stopped 1+ months:

  1. Re-take placement quiz (14-assessments.md)
  2. Review one module below your level (confidence)
  3. Start 12-week plan again at Week 1 rhythm — content may be faster second time

Shame is not a study method. Restart is.

Lesson 4 — Study space safety (15 min)

  • Chair or cushion, good light if possible
  • Phone on silent except study app
  • Water nearby
  • Tell one trusted person “I study now” if that protects your time
  • If interrupted often, use headphones + short sessions

Digital: separate folder for English; avoid mixing with stressful chats mid-session.

Lesson 5 — Sister study circle (optional, 20 min)

Safe circle rules:

  • 2–4 trusted women only
  • Meet by voice/chat at home — no unsafe travel required
  • Agenda: 30 min English only
  • Rotate: one teaches vocabulary, one asks questions
  • No criticism of accent — one correction per person per session
  • Share resources, not personal danger stories

Weekly format:

MinActivity
5Warm-up: each says one sentence
10Quiz each other on words
10Read one paragraph aloud
5Plan homework for alone days

Lesson 6 — Anti-burnout rules (10 min)

  • One day off per week is OK
  • Max 45 min if tired — quality over quantity
  • Switch skill if bored (grammar → listening)
  • Sleep and food matter for memory
  • Celebrate months studied, not only level jumps

Lesson 7 — Common learning myths (10 min)

MythTruth
“Adults cannot learn”Adults learn well with strategy
“Accent must be perfect”Clarity beats accent
“Grammar first, speak later”Speak small from day 1
“One app is enough”Rotate skills and sources
“Missing a day = failure”Habit returns with one session

Examples

Monday self-talk after a hard week:

I stopped for five days. That is human. Today I review ten words and listen to one clip. I am still a learner. I am still moving forward.

Sister circle message:

Sisters, tomorrow 4 p.m. we practice English 30 minutes. Bring three new words. We will read paragraph 2 from Reading module together.

Practice tasks

  1. Write your why in 2–3 sentences.
  2. Plan your safe study corner (draw or list).
  3. If you had a break, do 15-minute restart today.
  4. Optional: invite one trusted friend to 30-min English chat.

Self-check questions

  1. What are the 3 pillars?
  2. What should you do after a 2-week break?
  3. Name two sister-circle safety rules.
  4. Why is shame harmful to learning?
  5. What is a good goal for a tired day?

Assessments and Progress Checkpoints

All stages · Try first · Keys in answer-keys/

Learning goals

  1. Find your starting level (placement)
  2. Check progress at 4 checkpoints
  3. Score speaking and writing with rubrics
  4. Know when to move up a stage
  5. Use results to adjust your study plan

Why assessment matters

Tests are not punishment. They are a mirror — they show what to study next.

A low score today is data, not destiny.

Placement quiz (25 questions)

Instructions: 45 minutes max. No dictionary. No answer keys until finished.

Check answers: answer-keys/14-placement-quiz-KEYS.md

Part A — Foundations (1–8)

  1. Write the English alphabet in order (or recite and write).
  2. What is 14 in English words?
  3. Complete: She ___ a student. (am / is / are)
  4. Complete: They ___ English every day. (study / studies)
  5. Negative: He likes tea. → He ___ ___ tea.
  6. Question: You live in Kabul. → ___ you live in Kabul?
  7. Choose: There ___ two books. (is / are)
  8. Translate idea to English (write one sentence): I thank you.

Part B — Grammar & vocab (9–16)

  1. Past of go: ___
  2. Complete: I ___ studying when she called. (was / were / am)
  3. Future: I ___ visit tomorrow. (will / am will / wills)
  4. Modal: You ___ rest if you are sick. (should / must both OK — choose one)
  5. Opposite of hot: ___
  6. Write one sentence with because.
  7. Article: I need ___ pen. (a / an / the)
  8. Write 5 words on the topic study.

Part C — Intermediate (17–22)

  1. Past participle used with have: I have ___ (finish) → ___
  2. Second conditional: If I ___ (have) more time, I ___ (study) more.
  3. Relative clause: The woman ___ teaches online is kind. (who / which)
  4. Formal: Choose better: I want help OR I would like assistance
  5. Write 2 sentences: past + future about your English learning.
  6. Connector: Complete with one word: I was tired, ___ I continued.

Part D — Advanced (23–25)

  1. Passive: English ___ (teach) in many countries.
  2. Hedge: Rewrite softer: English is easy. → ___
  3. Write 3–4 sentences: opinion on why education matters for women.

Progress Tracker

Track small wins. Numbers are proof that you are moving forward.

Learning goals

By using this tracker weekly, you will:

  1. Build a daily study habit (even 15 minutes counts)
  2. See which skills you practice most and least
  3. Set realistic monthly goals
  4. Recover motivation after breaks
  5. Prepare for checkpoint quizzes in 14-assessments.md

Why tracking matters

When life is hard, memory lies. You forget that you learned 200 words last month. A tracker shows the truth: you are growing.

What gets measured gets improved — but kindness matters more than perfection.

Level focus

All stages (0–4). Update checklists as you advance.

Stage checklists

Stage 0 — Pre-beginner

  • I know the English alphabet
  • I can type my name on a phone keyboard
  • I know 50 survival words
  • I can say: Hello, Thank you, My name is…

Stage 1 — Beginner (A1–A2)

  • I use present tense for daily habits
  • I know 300+ common words
  • I can write a 5-sentence message about my day
  • I understand slow English (clear audio)
  • I studied 60+ days total (not necessarily in a row)

Stage 2 — Intermediate (B1)

  • I use past tense for stories
  • I know 800+ words
  • I can write one paragraph (80+ words)
  • I understand normal-speed short videos with some repetition
  • I speak 2 minutes without switching to Dari

Stage 3 — Upper intermediate (B2–C1)

  • I use conditionals and complex sentences
  • I read news or blog articles with a dictionary sometimes
  • I write formal emails
  • I understand podcasts with focus

Stage 4 — Advanced (C1–C2)

  • I argue a position politely in English
  • I write clear multi-paragraph essays
  • I adjust tone (formal vs friendly)
  • I learn new topics primarily through English

راهنمای هزارگی — Hazaragi Guide Introduction

English for Hazara Speakers — Learning American English from a Hazaragi Perspective

Welcome — خوش آمدید

This is the first comprehensive Hazaragi companion guide for learning English. Hazaragi is a rich, valid language variety spoken by millions of Hazara people, primarily in Afghanistan (Hazarajat region) and diaspora communities worldwide.

This guide recognizes:

  • Hazaragi is not "broken Dari" — it's a distinct dialect with its own phonology, vocabulary, and grammar
  • Hazaragi speakers face unique pronunciation challenges when learning English
  • Common Hazaragi words and expressions deserve direct translation, not only Dari bridges
  • Accent notes: "how we say it" in Hazaragi vs Standard Dari vs English

Who This Guide Is For

  • Hazara women and girls learning English at home
  • Speakers of Hazaragi (هزارگی) as first or heritage language

Hazaragi Pronunciation Guide for English Learners

Specific challenges and solutions for Hazaragi speakers learning American English

Overview

Hazaragi and English have very different sound systems. This guide identifies the key pronunciation challenges you'll face and gives you specific practice drills.

Good news: Once you train your ear and mouth for these sounds, English pronunciation becomes much clearer.

Vowel Challenges

Challenge 1: Hazaragi Vowel System is Simpler

Hazaragi vowels: ~4-5 main vowels (a, e/i, o, u) English vowels: 12-15 distinct vowel sounds

Impact: Many English word pairs sound the same to Hazaragi speakers at first.

English Vowel Pairs That Confuse Hazaragi Speakers

English PairHazaragi TendencyFix
ship /ɪ/ vs sheep /iː/Both sound like iship = short, relaxed; sheep = long, tense
cat /æ/ vs cut /ʌ/Both sound like acat = front, wide mouth; cut = central, relaxed
bet /ɛ/ vs bat /æ/Both sound like e or abet = mid; bat = low, open
pull /ʊ/ vs pool /uː/Both sound like upull = short; pool = long
cot /ɑ/ vs caught /ɔ/Both sound like o (merged in some US accents)Don't worry too much — many Americans merge these too

Practice Drill: Vowel Awareness

Week 1: Listen and repeat 10 times each day

  • ship — sheep (short i vs long ee)
  • sit — seat
  • hit — heat

Week 2:

  • cat — cut (front a vs central u)
  • bat — but

Week 3:

  • bet — bat (mid e vs low a)
  • pen — pan

Consonant Challenges

Challenge 2: English Consonants Not in Hazaragi

TH Sounds (Voiced & Voiceless)

Hazaragi: No th sound. Speakers often replace with t, d, s, or z.

English th types:

  1. Voiceless /θ/ — think, thank, math

    • Hazaragi speakers often say: tink, tank, mat
    • Fix: Put tongue lightly between teeth, blow air out (no voice)

  2. Voiced /ð/ — this, that, mother

    • Hazaragi speakers often say: dis, dat, mudder
    • Fix: Same tongue position, but add voice (feel vibration in throat)

Practice:

  • thank you (not tank you)
  • this is (not dis is)
  • mother (not mudder)

Drill: Say 20 times slowly every day for one week:

  • thank — that — think — this

W vs V

Hazaragi: w sound exists (و) but v is less common or borrowed.

English distinction:

  • W /w/: Round lips, no teeth — water, we, west
  • V /v/: Top teeth on bottom lip — very, vote, video

Hazaragi mistake: very → sounds like wery

Practice:

  • west (lips round) vs vest (teeth on lip)
  • wine vs vine
  • we vs V (letter)

Drill: Alternate 10 times:

  • very — west — video — water

R Sound

Hazaragi: Trilled or tapped r (like ř in some dialects) American English: Retroflex r (tongue curls back, doesn't tap palate)

Hazaragi mistake: red sounds like řed (with a trill/tap)

Fix:

  • Curl tongue back (tip doesn't touch roof)
  • Make a continuous sound: rrrrr (no taps)

Practice words:

  • red, right, run, mother, water, far

P, T, K (Aspiration)

Hazaragi: p, t, k are not strongly aspirated (no puff of air) English: Word-initial p, t, k have a puff of air

Test: Hold paper in front of mouth. Say pet — paper should move from air puff.

Hazaragi tendency: pet sounds like bet (no aspiration)

Fix: Exaggerate the puff of air at first

Practice:

  • pin, please, pretty
  • top, time, take
  • cat (= /k/), keep, come

Hazaragi-Specific Consonant Features

Hazaragi ř (Rolled/Trilled R)

Your advantage: You can roll r! This is harder for English speakers to learn.

For English: Don't roll it. Use American r (retroflex, no tongue tap).

Practice:

  • Hazaragi beřem (I go) → roll OK
  • English red → NO roll, tongue curled back

Hazaragi ğ (Voiced Velar Fricative)

Hazaragi: bağ (garden), dağ (mountain) have a soft, back-of-throat ğ

English equivalent: Like g in Spanish amigo (soft, not hard g)

English challenge: English doesn't have this sound. Closest is:

  • Soft g in get (but English g is a stop, not fricative)
  • Just use regular English g — it's fine

No drill needed — English g is simpler than Hazaragi ğ.

Word Stress & Rhythm

Hazaragi Stress Patterns

Hazaragi: Often stress-timed, but more flexible than English.

English: Strict stress patterns — wrong stress = confusing word.

English Stress Rules (Simplified)

  1. Two-syllable nouns: Usually stress first syllable

    • TA-ble, WA-ter, MOTH-er

  2. Two-syllable verbs: Often stress second syllable

    • be-GIN, re-PEAT, for-GET

  3. Longer words: Stress usually 2nd or 3rd syllable from end

    • pho-TO-graphy, com-PU-ter, edu-CA-tion

Hazaragi Mistake: Flat Stress

Hazaragi speakers often stress every syllable equally.

English: One syllable is LOUD and long; others are quiet and short.

Practice: Clap on the stressed syllable

  • com-PU-ter (clap on PU)
  • ENG-lish (clap on ENG)
  • to-MOR-row (clap on MOR)

Drill: Say these with exaggerated stress 10 times:

  • TEA-cher
  • STU-dent
  • im-POR-tant
  • be-CAUSE

Hazaragi Vowel Mergers in English

E / I Merger

Hazaragi: e and i often sound very similar (especially in fast speech)

English: They are distinct sounds.

English WordHazaragi TendencyFix
sit (short i)Sounds like setTongue higher, mouth more closed
seat (long ee)Sounds like sit or setTongue highest, longest vowel
set (short e)OKTongue mid-height

Practice:

  • sit — set — seat (all different!)
  • in — pen — seen

O / U in Unstressed Syllables

Hazaragi: o and u in unstressed syllables often merge

English: Both become schwa /ə/ (the lazy "uh" sound)

Example:

  • about → a-BOUT (not a-boot or a-boat)
  • sofa → SO-fuh (not so-fa clearly)

This is actually easier! English unstressed vowels are lazy too.

Sentence Rhythm & Intonation

Hazaragi Sentence Melody

Hazaragi: More melodic, rises and falls smoothly

English: Choppier — strong beats on content words, weak beats on function words.

English Sentence Stress Pattern

Strong (stressed): Nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, question words Weak (unstressed): Articles (a, the), prepositions (in, on), pronouns (he, she, it)

Example:

I WANT to STUDY ENG-lish at HOME.

Not:

I WANT TO STUD-Y ENG-LISH AT HOME. (every word stressed — sounds robotic)

Practice: Say naturally, with rhythm:

  • She LIVES in KaBUL.
  • I NEED HELP with GRAM-mar.
  • WHERE is the BOOK?

Study Tips: Hazaragi → English

  1. Don't translate word-for-word

    • Hazaragi: SOV (Subject-Object-Verb)

    • Example: "I book read" → "I read a book"

  2. Add articles (a, an, the)

    • Hazaragi has no articles
    • English requires them: "I have book" → "I have a book"

  3. English needs pronouns as subjects

    • Hazaragi: Mī-khōnam (verb alone, "I read/am reading")
    • English: I read (subject required)

  4. Hazaragi plurals are flexible; English plurals are strict

    • Hazaragi: mardim can mean "people" (already plural)
    • English: man → men, woman → women (irregular)

Hazaragi vs Dari: Key Differences

Understanding the relationship between Hazaragi, Standard Dari, and your English learning

Overview

Hazaragi and Dari are closely related but distinct language varieties within the Persian language family. Understanding the differences helps you:

  1. Read Dari-script materials while thinking in Hazaragi
  2. Avoid confusion when using Dari resources
  3. Feel confident that Hazaragi is valid, not "wrong Dari"

Persian Language Family

    └── Tajik (Tajikistan Persian)

Hazaragi is sometimes called:

  • A dialect of Dari
  • A separate language
  • Hazara Dari
  • Azaragi (older term)

Pronoun Dropping

Both Hazaragi and Dari allow pro-drop (omitting subject pronouns), but Hazaragi does it more frequently:

Hazaragi:

Mī-berem. ("Am going" — no "I")

Dari:

(Man) mī-rawam. ("I am going" — optional "I")

English:

"I am going." (Subject required!)

4. Syntax (Word Order)

Both Hazaragi and Dari use SOV (Subject-Object-Verb):

Hazaragi & Dari:

Man ketow mī-khōnam. (I book read.)

English uses SVO:

I read a book. (I read book.)

For English learners: You must switch your word order!

Hazaragi culture is collective, not individualistic.

Use this for English:

  • Teach what you learn (teaching strengthens your own knowledge)
  • Think of English as a tool to help your community, not just yourself

Strength 3: Practical Skills

Hazara people are known for practical problem-solving (agriculture, crafts, trade).

Use this for English:

  • Learn English through practical activities: cooking (recipe vocab), shopping (money vocab), health (body parts, symptoms)

Challenge 1: Limited Formal Schooling

Impact on English learning:

  • Unfamiliar with "school" learning style (grammar rules, exercises)

  2. Treat Latin alphabet as a completely new system
    • Don't compare to Perso-Arabic; learn from scratch

  3. Use audio resources heavily

  4. Grammar comes later
    • Learn useful phrases first: "I need help." "Where is the bathroom?"

Feeling: "I'm too old. I should have learned English in school."

Reality: Many successful English learners started as adults. Age is not the barrier — consistency is the key.

Solution:

  1. Reframe the narrative:

    • Not: "I'm behind."
    • Instead: "I'm starting now, which is brave."

  2. Compare to yesterday, not to others

  3. Remember: Adults learn differently (better in some ways!)

Impact: Hard to find 1-2 hours for study.

Hazaragi-Specific Grammar Tips

Tip 1: English Needs Subject Pronouns

Hazaragi: Mī-berem. (verb alone = "I go")

English: Must say "I go" (subject required)

Practice: Add I, you, he, she, we, they to every sentence when translating from Hazaragi.

Tip 2: English Word Order is Strict (SVO)

Hazaragi: Flexible SOV

English: Must be SVO

Hazaragi thinking:

Man ketow (O) mī-khōnam (V). (I book read.)

English requires:

I (S) read (V) a book (O).

Practice: When translating from Hazaragi, rearrange: Subject first, then verb, then object.

Tip 3: Articles (a, an, the) Are Required

Hazaragi: No articles

English: Almost every noun needs a, an, or the

Hazaragi:

Ketow dar mēz ast. (Book on table is.)

English:

The book is on the table. (Articles added)

Practice: Every time you say a noun in English, ask: "Does it need a/an/the?"

Contributing and Expanding This Roadmap

Thank you for helping learners. This is a content repository (Markdown), not an app.

How to add content safely

  1. Keep American English spelling.
  2. Keep tone motivational and respectful for young women in Afghanistan.
  3. Avoid political campaigns, unsafe meetup advice, or shaming language.
  4. Do not put answer keys inside student modules — use answer-keys/.
  5. Add Dari glosses only in appendices/dari-glosses.md unless one line in a module is essential.
